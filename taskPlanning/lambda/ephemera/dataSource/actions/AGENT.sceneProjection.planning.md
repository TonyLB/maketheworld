## Goal

Every LLM rung still to be built (the orchestration loop in the [iteration ladder's proposal](AGENT.objectManipulationIterations.planning.md#proposal-deterministic-first-then-an-orchestration-loop-2026-10-25), the deferred adjudication tier's judge, the synthesis fallback) has to reason about what the actor can reach. The `ludicCache` holds that, but in a shape that is very bad for semantic reasoning. Membership is node-list position, not an edge. A hosting kind sits on a cover entry, not on a relation. Where a thing sits is a walk over `fromHostPresences`. One tie is three edges (`Custom`, the `SecuredTo` it justifies, and the `Justifies` between them). Handing the model JSON makes it reconstruct those facts before it can reason, and switches register between ids and meaning on every line.

**The reader the scene is designed for is the orchestration loop** (user, 2026-11-02). There, nothing is identified yet: the loop identifies things itself (Identify is one of its tools), reasoning over the scene and naming things by handle. The adjudication judge is a secondary consumer: it reads an attempt the deterministic pipeline already grounded, so it adds a question built from that attempt, which the loop doesn't have (SP-11). Design choices here answer to the loop's needs first.

This plan builds an **output-only projection** from the actor's cache into text an LLM reads as a scene:

```text
Contents of the Lab:
- [pole1] Pole (a tarred mooring post)
- [rope1] Rope (a coil of thick hemp rope), tied to [pole1]
```

It has two layers:

- **A scene model:** structured data built from the actor's cache. It holds the described things, their handles, where each is placed, and the relations between them as phrases. Beside it sits a **handle table** mapping each handle to an id, which is never shown to the model.
- **A text renderer** over the scene model. Several candidate renderers are built first and compared by a hands-on Bedrock probe (Slice 3); the losers are deleted and one renderer ships.

"Output-only" means the model is never parsed back into the cache. The handle table is how a later reader's output names things again (see SP-1).

**Payoff, at the observable output:** in dev, a player types `/test scene` and receives, as an OOC message, the rendered scene of their own actor's cache. That covers the room's contents, nested, with relations in words, and their held inventory. The integration test ends at that published message, not at the scene model. A second, smaller payoff is that the judge reads the scene and a question built from its attempt, in place of `CommandAttempt.renderProse`'s six-section format and its string-list stub (Slice 6, SP-11).

## Candidate formats

**Decided (SP-7, user, 2026-11-03): D ships. Reopened the same day (user) against `fans`, below.** The other candidates and their goldens are deleted; they stay described below as the record of what was compared and why. Code for the two live candidates: [`renderSceneTree.ts`](../../../../../lambda/ephemera/dataSource/actions/sceneProse/renderSceneTree.ts) (D) and [`renderSceneFans.ts`](../../../../../lambda/ephemera/dataSource/actions/sceneProse/renderSceneFans.ts).

**Why compare (user, 2026-11-02).** English is much closer than JSON to the register the reader reasons in, but it is bad at nesting. More precisely, it is bad at branching. A right-branching chain reads easily ("the cup on the saucer on the table in the tavern"). A fan of siblings inside another fan does not: after a subtree, prose has no clean way to say which level it has returned to. Indentation is the usual patch. Whether an LLM reads indentation reliably is a hypothesis, not a fact. Finding a line's parent from indentation means comparing leading whitespace, which is a counting task over tokens that split inconsistently. Matching an identical handle string earlier in the context is what attention does best. So the candidates differ in which cue carries the structure: whitespace, a lexical parent clause, or neither.

The worked scene used below: a tavern (with gloss) as the room; a table holding a saucer with a cup on it, and a candle; the actor holding a hammer. The candle is there so every format has to climb back out of a subtree.

**A. Indented tree.** Hosting kinds are carried by indentation alone.

```text
The Tavern: A low-beamed common room, smoky from the hearth.
Contents of the Tavern:
- [table1] Table
  - [saucer1] Saucer
    - [cup1] Cup
  - [candle1] Candle
You are holding:
- [hammer1] Hammer
```

**D. Indented tree with a parent clause.** Every nested line names its parent by handle, with the hosting kind in words. The indentation is a visual aid; the clause carries the meaning.

```text
- [table1] Table, in the Tavern
  - [saucer1] Saucer, on [table1]
    - [cup1] Cup, on [saucer1]
  - [candle1] Candle, on [table1]
```

**Blocks.** Shape-aware, with no indentation. The renderer analyses the physical forest and uses English's two native shapes: a host with children is a block ("On [table1]: ..."); a child whose contents form a chain (one thing per level) stays inline as a phrase read downward from the child; a child that branches (two or more things) is promoted to its own block and marked "(see below)" in its parent's list. The deepest structure is one list plus inline phrases, and every jump between blocks is a handle match. The number of blocks grows with how much the scene branches, not with its depth.

```text
In the Tavern: [table1] Table.
On [table1]: [saucer1] Saucer, with [cup1] Cup on it; [candle1] Candle.
You are holding: [hammer1] Hammer.

(with a tray holding a teapot and a jug also on the table)
On [table1]: [saucer1] Saucer, with [cup1] Cup on it; [candle1] Candle; [tray1] Tray (see below).
On [tray1]: [teapot1] Teapot; [jug1] Jug.
```

Known costs of Blocks: the render's shape flips when the scene changes (a spoon put on the saucer promotes it to a block), so across a loop's turns one object can be rendered two ways, though its handle is stable; and each hosting kind needs chain wording ("with X on it", "with X in it", "with X as a part").

**blocksD: Blocks' grouping with D's lines** (user, 2026-11-02, after the first probe batches; see SP-7). The reading: Blocks' strength is *associating* the right things (a block gathers a host's members), and its weakness is *disassociating* them on structural questions (an inline chain read as the outer host's member; a line with no local placement cue). So every item is a D-style row naming its parent, with no inline chains. A member with exactly one thing below it, and nothing below that (a one-deep chain), has that thing hoisted into the same block as its own row, its clause naming its real parent; anything more (a fan, or a chain two or more deep) is marked `(see below)` and gets its own block. Members of different hosting kinds get a block each, as in Blocks; a relation never promotes, since no row is inline. With tea in the cup:

```text
In the Tavern:
- [table1] Table, in the Tavern (see below)
On [table1]:
- [saucer1] Saucer, on [table1] (see below)
- [candle1] Candle, on [table1]
On [saucer1]:
- [cup1] Cup, on [saucer1]
- [tea1] Tea, in [cup1]
```

Its known risk: a hoisted row sits under a header that is not its host (`On [saucer1]:` heads the tea), so the clause must beat the header; fixture `sideboard` asks exactly that (`directContents` of the saucer and the mug). The room is a list block like the others, not Blocks' one-line room. Code: [`renderSceneBlocksD.ts`](../../../../../lambda/ephemera/dataSource/actions/sceneProse/renderSceneBlocksD.ts).

**blocksDLabelled: every header true of every row under it** (user, 2026-11-02, after the `sideboard` batches; see SP-7). The `sideboard` batches showed blocksD's known risk come true: the model reads a header as membership over a hoisted row's own clause. So nothing is hoisted, and each header says what kind of block it is (the user's proposal: label the shapes; the wording states the reading rule rather than naming the shape). A **fan block** holds a host's direct members, `Directly on [x]:` or `Directly in [x]:` (other hosting kinds keep their headers, which are already direct). A member with anything below it is marked `(see below)` and gets its own block, in pre-order. A **single-file run** at least two deep (one thing, one thing on or in that, and so on) is a **layers block** of D-style rows, `[x] and what is on or in it, in layers:`; where the run ends in a fan, that thing is marked `(see below)` and gets a fan block. A one-deep chain is a fan block of one row. On `sideboard`:

```text
Directly on [sideboard1]:
- [candle1] Candle, on [sideboard1]
- [saucer1] Saucer, on [sideboard1] (see below)
- [mug1] Mug, on [sideboard1] (see below)
- [tray1] Tray, on [sideboard1] (see below)
- [box1] Box, on [sideboard1] (see below)
- [lamp1] Lamp, on [sideboard1]
[saucer1] and what is on or in it, in layers:
- [cup1] Cup, on [saucer1]
- [tea1] Tea, in [cup1]
Directly in [mug1]:
- [coffee1] Coffee, in [mug1]
```

Known gap: a join (fixture `join`) sits in the block of its last host's binding, so `Directly on [table-b1]:` heads a fruit bowl that is on the tablecloth; the scene model places a join there for every format, and no probe batch has asked about it yet. Code (deleted with SP-7's first decision): `renderSceneBlocksDLabelled.ts`, at commit `30ed8e4cc`.

**`fans`: grouping with no shape analysis** (user, 2026-11-03, reopening SP-7). blocksDLabelled without its layers block: every host with members gets one block per hosting kind (`Directly on [x]:`, `Directly in [x]:`, `Parts of [x]:`), each row a D-style row naming its parent, and a member with anything below it marked `(see below)`, its block following in pre-order. A chain is a block per link, so nothing depends on a thing's shape, depth is stated by every row's clause, and every header is true of every row under it. This is the "fan-blocks-only variant" SP-7 named after the blocksDLabelled batch and held; it was never built before the decision. Its goldens are blocksDLabelled's with each layers block rewritten (`deepChain`, `sideboard`, `teaTable`); every other fixture renders as blocksDLabelled did. Members of another hosting kind get their block after the first kind's blocks and all their sub-blocks (shape test), which may put `Parts of [x]:` far from `Directly on [x]:`. On `sideboard`:

```text
Directly on [sideboard1]:
- [candle1] Candle, on [sideboard1]
- [saucer1] Saucer, on [sideboard1] (see below)
- [mug1] Mug, on [sideboard1] (see below)
...
Directly on [saucer1]:
- [cup1] Cup, on [saucer1] (see below)
Directly in [cup1]:
- [tea1] Tea, in [cup1]
Directly in [mug1]:
- [coffee1] Coffee, in [mug1]
```

**`flatD`: D with no indentation** (user, 2026-11-03). D's lines in D's pre-order, every line flush left. The user's worry: an indented tree may bring a YAML or tree-manipulation register with it, so a model reasons about nodes and children instead of the things in the room. D's clauses already state every parent, so the indent carries no fact of its own; dropping it removes the cue and changes nothing else. If flatD beats D on the questions where answers drift from the physical facts, the indent costs something; if they tie, it is decoration. Unlike the rejected flat-chain form below, each line names only its parent. Goldens are D's with each line's leading whitespace stripped (`sceneFlatDGoldens`, derived, since that is flatD's whole spec). Free check alongside it: scan earlier batches' reasoning for tree vocabulary ("child", "nested", "node"). On `sideboard`:

```text
- [saucer1] Saucer, on [sideboard1]
- [cup1] Cup, on [saucer1]
- [tea1] Tea, in [cup1]
- [mug1] Mug, on [sideboard1]
```

## Frame and answer-format findings (Slice 5, 2026-11-03)

The evidence behind the frame and answer-format changes (the renders' first comparison, the direction audit, each frame edit, the answer-format table, the renders under the current frame, the blocker ladder) is in [the discussion record](AGENT.sceneProjection.discussion.planning.md#frame-and-answer-format-findings-moved-from-the-plan-2026-11-03). **Where it stands (2026-11-03):** the frame now has a grounding with a blocker ladder (simplest steps, then freeing one blocker, then puzzle-solver paths) and a precedence line by register (the scene wins on what is here and how it is placed, the world on what follows from it), a `MOVING` legend in two registers (results, then the records' edits), and an `answerFormats` axis with `twoRegisters` (default) and `abstain`. ## Prompt layers and the judge's input (2026-11-04)

**Status: a direction (user, 2026-11-04), not a result.** Decision row: SP-11. The oracle prototype is what proves it: the probe has asked feasibility questions, but never under a verdict answer format.

**A general, modular prompt.** Each LLM agent is given the same grounding for working with the representation, plus the instructions for its own job. The working frame held these pieces, tangled together, in [`sceneReasoningPrompt.ts`](../../../../../lambda/ephemera/calibration/sceneReasoning/sceneReasoningPrompt.ts); since 2026-11-04 they are separate modules in [`sceneProse/frame/`](../../../../../lambda/ephemera/dataSource/actions/sceneProse/frame/index.ts) (`frameReading`, `frameFiction`, `frameGraph`, `answerFormats`, `composeFrame`), with the wording unchanged:

| Layer | What it says | In the current frame | Who needs it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Reading | How to interpret a scene projection; tuned alongside each render | Grounding's first paragraph; the "scene, as I perceive it" line | Every reader |
| 2a. Acting in the fiction | What is reasonable to do and what stops it | Grounding's precedence paragraph; the blocker ladder; the fiction fact inside `MOVING`'s first paragraph (what is on the crate goes along, the cart does not) | Loop and judge |
| 2b. Changing the graph | How a result is recorded as edits | Most of `MOVING`, whose first paragraph is deliberately kept to the recording side; `NOTATION` | Loop only |
| Job | The question and what to answer | The question; the answer format, including `twoRegisters`' "What changes" | Per agent |

- **The 2a/2b line is provisional.** Layer 2 is the user's; the split is a proposal, and `MOVING` does not divide cleanly along it. Whether the judge needs all of the ladder is also open: "take the simplest way" is for a reader finding a plan, while a judge is handed the steps.
- **Layer 2 is tied to the render too.** `MOVING` says "the records", which only `registers` has as a heading (D has none), and "the same 'on' and 'in' the scene's lines use". Retuning one layer can invalidate batches run on another (SP-8, "Frame and layout interact").

**The scene replaces the six-section format (user, 2026-11-04).** That format was a prototype: no LLM has ever read it, CA-1 settled it from hand-written examples, it predates the orchestration loop, and its room context is a string-list stub. The scene is its next iteration, and each section has a better home:

| CA-1 section | Where it goes |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Player's words | The question |
| 2. Referents | Handles in the question and the scene; names and glosses are already on the scene's lines |
| 3. State | The scene, once a state axis exists ([questions](#questions-this-iteration-should-surface), "State") |
| 4. Room context | The scene |
| 5. Actions and challenges | The question, built from `action.describe()`, facilitating actions included |
| 6. Result | Dropped from the input: for a judge it is "pending", and the verdict is what the judge produces |

**Not quite drop-in:** the scene describes the world and says nothing about what is being attempted, so a small function turns the attempt into a question ([`attemptQuestion`](../../../../../lambda/ephemera/dataSource/actions/sceneProse/attemptQuestion.ts), 2026-11-04: `describe()` wording with the handle beside each name; challenges and the result left out). **What survives of CA-1:** prose is built only when an LLM will read it; scope is swappable (a wider scope is a different scene, not a different format); a gloss is a reasoning field.

**Starting hypothesis: same question, only the answer format changes.** "Could the actor pick up [cup1]?" is the question the probe already asks. Re-deriving graph edits would be wasted work on a simple state, but the frame is what requests them (`twoRegisters`' "What changes", `NOTATION`, `MOVING`). A judge's frame leaves those out, stops after "What happens", and adds a one-line verdict. (Refined 2026-11-04: the verdict is a triage label, `simple` / `complex` / `impossible`, and reasoning is kept short as a fast first check; see Slice 6, "The judge's answer format".) No fresh wording for "a proposed action with computed consequences" until a failure calls for it. Two checks:

- **Does the model still drift into edits** with nothing asking for them? Read it from the answers and the output tokens.
- **The different route.** The ladder can answer yes by a route the attempt does not contain: "yes, by moving the boulder off the rope", when the attempt only moves the rope. That approves a different graph change. Another route to the same result is fine ("different actions with the same result are the same change"), so the judge needs a way to say "yes, but something else has to change too": an answer-format line, not new reasoning instructions.

## Questions this iteration should surface

Not Open rows: none of these gates a slice. Each is recorded when Slice 0 or a later slice meets it, with the scene that raised it, and is handed to an owner in Slice 7 (an existing plan, a new plan, or a shelf in a durable doc).

- **Scope.** Is the whole actor's cache the right context, or a part scoped to the command (the things Identify's pools touched, plus their neighbours)? This iteration renders everything. The scope function in CA-1 is where a narrower scope goes, and attention history decides what the cache holds at all.
- **Size.** Token count of a rendered scene for the Coyote rooms at their placement cap. Measured in Slice 4 (the user's dev smoke test, 2026-11-03): the Cliff Base, four placements (a cup on a saucer on a table, and a ball of string), renders in D as 7 lines, 244 characters with D's indentation, about 75 tokens (estimated at about 3.3 characters a token, since bracketed, hyphenated handles split finely; no tokenizer was run). That is about 20 tokens of heading and holding line, plus about 13 tokens per placement line, so it grows linearly with the placement count. It decides nothing until a reader has a budget. The client drops leading whitespace by design, so the harness shows D unindented; the payoff test pins the indented string the reader is sent. The probe's renders give a per-format count for free, which may bear on SP-7 if the formats differ much.
- **Shape stability across turns.** If SP-1 makes handles last across a command's loop turns and SP-7 picks Blocks, one object can render as an inline chain on one turn and a block on the next. Whether that confuses a multi-turn reader is a question the single-prompt probe cannot answer.
- **Handles for the room and the actor.** If a reader's output must name "the hammer you hold" or "put it on the floor", the room and the actor need handles, and "you" cannot be the actor's only name. Belongs with SP-1. *Met in Slice 1:* a relation whose subject has no line (the actor, a bystander) is left out of the scene model, since there is no handle to hold it; a handle for the actor would bring those relations back.
- **Grounding a handle back.** How a reader's output names a handle so that it becomes a `ReferentAssignment` entry, or a `graphNode` referent, without breaking the freeze rule (actions contract, "Plan stays blind to the world, or its output is frozen whole").
- **The hand-up.** The loop's first turn needs the fills, readings, pools and abstain reasons beside the scene. Today `compileAttemptsFromSkeleton` returns one `Abstain { reason }`. That is a separate piece of foundation, not this plan's.
- **Unnamed things.** A node with no `shortName` is left out of the catalog, but it can still be one end of a relation or a container. *Slice 0 renders every unnamed node* as `[object1] (unnamed object)` (fixture `unnamed`); whether an unnamed leaf, neither container nor relation end, should be dropped is still open.
- **State.** The prose's State section overrides the gloss. Where the scene would carry a state override once a state axis exists. One case to meet then: things inside a closed container may need to be hidden or marked, since the actor may not perceive them.
- **Whether `readingLabel`'s "on the X" for every object parent should take the scene's wording** (one fix for both). The scene's hosting-kind words are built (`hostingClause` in `sceneWording.ts`: on, in, held by, part of, a section of); `objectViews.readingLabel` still says "on" for every kind.
- **Bystanders and features** as scene content. Bystanders are a plain question. **Narrowed (Slice 1, 2026-11-02):** features the cache holds are now placed like objects (SP-4 as amended).
