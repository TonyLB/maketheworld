A player's plan is being worked out one action at a time, and the action after "End of scene." needs to be imagined in more detail. Your goal is to describe, from what has been established about this place and what you know about the world, how that action would ordinarily be done, and to point out anything in the scene that might get in the way.

Reading the scene: The scene below is what has been established about this place. Some things are named by handles in square brackets, to show that they are established in the scene. In the scene, "you" is the player. It is shown two ways, each a view of the same place:
- "Fiction" describes what has been established about this place in the story, in sentences. Reasoning is grounded in this established fiction. Everything it states is true. What it doesn't state is not established as either present or absent. This place is fiction, not a simulation: what is established is how things relate to each other, not exactly where each thing is. An established fact stays true unless something gives you a reason to doubt it, and you don't need to show that it survives.
- "Graph" is how the game records that same place, as a list with one row for each thing. A row indented under another row is a thing on or in that other thing. It adds no facts of its own but can help disambiguate the language of the fiction section.
The scene tells you what is here and how it is placed. What things are like, and what they could physically be used for, comes from what you know about the world. Where the two disagree about what is here or how it is placed, the scene wins.

Relations and frames: Each thing has a frame, which contains whatever is on or in that thing. A thing's containment relation (in, on, held by) names the frame that thing occupies: a book on a shelf occupies the shelf's frame. Changing the containment relation changes the frame occupied. Frames nest, and a frame can itself move. Whether a thing has moved is always a question about the frame that thing occupies. Relations and positional language ("on", "beside", "glued to") say how the things in a frame relate to each other and to the thing whose frame it is. Relations other than containment leave the frame occupied as it is. An action can also move things it never mentions, and a thing moves relative to another whenever its position or its orientation relative to that other thing changes.
